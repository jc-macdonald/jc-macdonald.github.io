Joshua C. Macdonald

Assistant Scientist · Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health

Baltimore, Maryland

My overarching interest is how ecological and landscape change influences the movement and connectivity of hosts, pathogens and cultural traits, and through them the persistence and evolution of structured populations. My established work addresses multiscale infection dynamics, linking viral growth and immune responses within hosts to transmission between hosts, and cultural transmission. Landscape-scale questions are a developing direction. I build mechanistic models, inference methods and open research software for these questions and for decisions under uncertainty. Applications include infectious disease, public and global health and ecology. I also develop methods for incomplete multivariate data. Developing work includes scientific machine learning, the identifiability of models that couple biological scales, and the design of experiments, monitoring programs and studies with field, laboratory and domain partners.

I am an Assistant Scientist at the International Vaccine Access Center, Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health. With the Atlantic Coast Center for Infectious Disease Dynamics and Analytics (ACCIDDA), I develop infectious disease models and software for scenario projections and public health planning. I also collaborate with the Jolles Lab on foot-and-mouth disease in African buffalo and Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever in livestock and wildlife, including transmission between species and surveillance design.

Research questions

My research asks three questions, studied in systems that include foot-and-mouth disease in African buffalo, Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever in livestock and wildlife, and human cultural transmission. The research page describes each system.

  • How do processes within hosts shape transmission and persistence? My buffalo studies link virus growth and immune response to transmission between animals for three strains of the SAT serotypes of foot-and-mouth disease virus. SAT-1 was reported for the first time in nine countries from Egypt to Mongolia in 2025 and 2026 (WOAH).
  • How do landscape, movement and contact shape spread, persistence and divergence across populations? This is a developing direction; the published work so far concerns cultural variation across geography and language, and the rest is collaborative work in progress.
  • How should we measure, vaccinate and decide under uncertainty? I address this through surveillance, vaccination and scenario modeling.

Research in progress

My current methods research asks how measurements and scientific assumptions affect what we can learn about a system.

  • Inference and calibration. Estimating unknown model quantities and assessing fitting procedures with simulated data. The op_infer source is public; application materials remain private. Its current development and release status is maintained on the project page.
  • Incomplete data and latent structure. Investigating how many patterns can be distinguished from noise in incomplete data, building on VBPCApy and a 2024 preprint.
  • Constraint checks for predictions. Reporting whether a prediction satisfies stated scientific constraints. This is now being folded into the validation layer of the software rather than developed as a separate method.

Current public- and global-health application studies address dengue within-host dynamics, observation noise, outbreak vaccination and Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever surveillance. These studies apply the methods above; they do not define the methods’ scope. The summaries describe ongoing questions; public papers and artifacts are linked as they become available.

Software

I build reusable, cross-domain tools spanning model specification, simulation, inference, calibration and validation, observation and experimental design, and comparison of decisions under scientific and resource constraints. Projects use the pieces they need. OP System and OP Engine support spatial fields, nonlocal interactions, compartmental dynamics and stochastic reactions. The stack brings to Python the separation of model specification from numerical method that odin2 and dust2 provide in R and that Catalyst provides in Julia. A specification is compiled once, and the array library (NumPy, JAX or PyTorch) is chosen when the model is called; model authors can check a compiled model against equations they write independently. The same specifications run in operational scenario projections and in research analyses (how the stack compares).

Software and project descriptions cover the packages and their development stages. Computed examples include stochastic ensembles and a replicated design comparison, with their numerical inputs and checks. Figure downloads provide vector graphics and editable sources.
